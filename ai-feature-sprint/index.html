What is the FDE Sprint?

The FDE Sprint is Eupraxic's fixed-scope engagement in which a forward deployed engineer ships one AI workflow into your product in ten business days. It's built for founder-led B2B SaaS teams of 5 to 50 people, and the workflow runs on your data. It's a fixed price, $5,000, refunded in full if there's no working prototype by Day 5 or no working workflow by Day 10.

Copilot or autopilot

A copilot is a tool that helps a professional do the work faster. The professional stays responsible for the result, and your customer pays for it per seat. An autopilot does the work itself and hands back a finished result with a score. A person approves that result instead of producing it, and the buyer pays for the work.

I take the terms from Sequoia's essay "Services: The New Software" by Julien Bek (March 2026). In a Sprint I ship the autopilot version of one workflow your team already touches, running next to the product your users keep. I scope it on Day 1, prove it on your data by Day 5, and put it in your repo on Day 10.

In a first Sprint, a person still approves anything that leaves your company. Autonomy comes later, once the rubric scores hold.

What is a forward deployed AI engineer?

A forward deployed AI engineer works inside the customer's codebase and ships AI workflows into their product, instead of advising from outside. The term comes from Palantir. In 2026, OpenAI and Anthropic both built teams around it to deploy their models inside customers. The FDE Sprint is the founder-tier version of that work.

02 · The problem

Your team knows AI matters. The hard part is shipping the right thing.

Most SaaS teams are already experimenting: someone has tried Claude Code, built a prototype, or floated a chatbot. The experiments stall because putting AI to work in a product takes more than a demo. It takes a clear workflow, the right data, a narrow scope, and an implementation path the team can actually ship.

That's what the Sprint produces: one managed AI workflow with a clear use case, a review process, and a handoff your team can run with.

03 · The model

What is Forward Deployed AI Engineering?

Forward Deployed AI Engineering is a delivery model where an engineer embeds inside a customer's team and ships AI capabilities directly into the customer's product and codebase. The deliverable is working code in the customer's repo, not a deck about it. The term comes from Palantir, where it has been the dominant model for two decades. In May 2026, Anthropic announced a new AI services company with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs, in which its Applied AI engineers build Claude systems for mid-sized companies. Days later, OpenAI launched the OpenAI Deployment Company, with more than $4 billion of investment, to place Forward Deployed Engineers inside customer organizations. The FDE Sprint is the same playbook, sized for founder-led B2B SaaS teams of 5 to 50 people who can't run a quarter-million-dollar procurement process.

04 · Example workflows

Examples of narrow AI workflows

  • Investor update assistant. Reads the month's metrics from Stripe or your finance sheet, plus a few sentences from the founder, and drafts the update in your usual format. The founder edits and sends.
  • Runway scenario explainer. Takes a proposed change (a hire, a price change, a churn assumption), runs it against current burn and revenue, and writes a plain-language note on what it does to runway. Someone checks the numbers before anyone acts on them.
  • Customer summary generator. Pulls an account's CRM notes, usage events, and open tickets, and writes a one-page summary into the CRM record before a renewal call. The account owner reads it before the call.
  • Support ticket summarizer. Reads a long Zendesk or Intercom thread and writes the issue, urgency, what has been tried, and a suggested next step into the ticket sidebar. The agent accepts it, edits it, or ignores it.
  • Report generator. Turns a week of product data or notes into your weekly customer or internal report, in your template, as a draft. Whoever sends it reviews it first.
  • Onboarding assistant. Uses a new account's setup data to suggest the next step in your product's first key workflow, inside the app. It suggests; the user clicks.

What the workflow is worth to you

The Sprint has a fixed price. What the workflow is worth depends on your numbers, so we work them out on the Scoping Call and write them into the Brief.

For an internal workflow the math is short: the hours a week the work takes today, times the loaded hourly cost of the people doing it, times 52. That's what the work costs you in a year. Divide the Sprint price by what the workflow saves each week, and you have the payback period.

Worked example, with example inputs. Four support agents each spend ten hours a week reading long ticket threads before they can reply. A summarizer that halves that saves 20 hours a week. At a loaded cost of $40 an hour, that's $800 a week, or $41,600 a year. The Sprint pays for itself in under ten weeks at either published price.

For a customer-facing workflow, hours saved is the wrong measure. The value is in the deals the workflow helps close or the accounts it helps keep, and the Brief names that metric instead. If neither number is bigger than the Sprint price, I'll say so on the call.

08 · How it works

How the 10-business-day sprint works

Day 1: Scope
Define the workflow, user problem, available data, constraints, and success criteria.
Days 2 to 3: Design
Map the UX flow, architecture, prompt and data structure, and implementation plan.
Days 4 to 5: Prototype
Build the first working version and validate the direction.
Days 6 to 9: Implementation
Refine the workflow, handle edge cases, add error states, improve prompts, prepare for handoff or deployment.
Day 10: Handoff
Staging deployment, production-ready pull request, or implementation handoff, depending on your infrastructure and access.

Why Day 1 decides the Sprint

I learned this on my own product first. The first AI workflows I built for Vectig were based on the wrong hypothesis: they solved a problem founders didn't actually have. It took conversations with founders, more research, and several pivots before the workflow pointed at something they really need.

Most stalled AI projects go wrong the same way, before any code is written. So the Sprint starts by pinning the workflow down in one signed sentence, with the person who'll use it, and nothing gets built until that sentence holds.

How a Sprint gets scoped on Day 1

Every Sprint Brief is written around a single sentence:

For [user], when [trigger or input] happens, the workflow will [process or action] and produce [output], so that [business value].

Worked example. "For the founder, when monthly metrics and 3 to 5 sentences of context are added, the workflow will draft a clean investor update covering metrics, risks, and asks, so that the founder can send consistent updates in 8 minutes instead of 90."

Under the sentence goes one value line: the hours a week the work takes today, times the loaded hourly cost, times 52. For a customer-facing workflow, it's the deal or retention metric it moves.

Value line for the example. 82 minutes saved a month is not where the money is. The value is an update that goes out every month, on time, which keeps investors close before the next round.

If we can't agree on the words that fill those five brackets on Day 1, the Sprint isn't ready to start.

The output quality rubric

Every Sprint ships with a 1 to 5 rubric tuned to its specific dimensions. The scale is fixed; the dimensions change per workflow.

ScoreMeaning
1Unusable. Output is wrong, incoherent, or unsafe.
2Needs major rewrite. Shape is right; substance requires substantial editing.
3Usable with significant edits. Team starts from this draft but invests real time refining.
4Good with minor edits. Small adjustments only. Usable in roughly the time it takes to read.
5Ready to use. No edits needed. Ships as-is.

Each workflow's rubric specifies its own dimensions: accuracy, clarity, tone, format, completeness. Each comes with worked examples at every score level.

What we'll take on as a first Sprint

  • Low risk

    Internal-only workflows. Drafts and summaries reviewed by a human before anything leaves the company. Most first Sprints sit here, and they should.

  • Medium risk

    Workflows touching customer-facing data (support tickets, CRM notes, sales transcripts, internal financial data) with explicit human review.

  • High risk

    Legal, medical, compliance, payments, or anything that takes customer-facing actions autonomously. Declined as a first Sprint. We'll help redefine to a lower-risk scope, or wait until trust and tooling are mature.

What is a 10-day FDE Sprint?

A 10-business-day engagement to ship one narrow AI workflow into a B2B SaaS product. Scoping on Day 1, design on Days 2 to 3, prototype by Day 5, implementation on Days 6 to 9, handoff on Day 10. How long does it take to ship an AI feature in a SaaS product?

Ten business days for one narrow workflow, if codebase access and sample data are ready on Day 1. What most teams call an AI feature, I scope as a workflow: one trigger in, one scored output out. Day 1 scopes it, Days 2 and 3 design it, a working prototype runs on your data by Day 5, and Day 10 is handoff. A broader project takes longer; the Sprint ships its first narrow slice.

How do I know if my AI feature idea is feasible?

Treat the idea as a workflow rather than an AI feature, and it's feasible in ten days if one person runs it, its data sits in one system you can export, it fits your existing APIs, and a person reviews the output before anything leaves. The Workflow Scorecard checks this in two minutes across eight dimensions and four readiness checks.

Who is this for?

Founder-led B2B SaaS companies with 5 to 50 employees, post-product-market-fit, with paying customers, real data inside their product, and pressure to put AI to work in their product. Not for indie hackers, solopreneurs, or enterprises with formal procurement.

What is the difference between a copilot tool and an autopilot workflow?

A copilot helps one of your users do the work and leaves them responsible for the result. An autopilot does the work and hands back a finished result with a score, which a person approves instead of producing. I ship the second kind, for one workflow, next to the product your users keep using.

Why hire an independent FDE instead of a model vendor's deployment team?

A vendor's forward deployed engineers work for the vendor, so the natural outcome is more of that vendor's platform and more spend on it. An independent FDE picks the model per workflow, keeps prompts and model settings in a config file your team controls, and can switch models when a better or cheaper one ships. Vendor teams also start at enterprise minimums; the FDE Sprint is sized for a 5 to 50 person company.
